The European consumer is more cautious. Italy is stalled on goods.

Alberto Maria GizziStrategy executive, consumer and luxuryData from primary sources · Method

Commentary. The European consumer has become more cautious. With inflation at 4.0%, driven by energy costs, a significant part of sales growth is price, not quantity: in Italy and Germany, the two weakest markets, growth is entirely in value terms, with volumes slightly down (in Italy +1.0% in value against −0.5% in volume, excluding fuel). Confidence, which collapsed in spring, has fallen again in September and once more in October, with Italy the most pessimistic (−23.3). And where savings are lowest, in Italy and Spain (between 9.5% and 11% of income, against 16.9–19% in France and Germany), there is less room to absorb price increases.

In Italy growth has stalled (third-quarter GDP 0.0%): household spending is rising only slightly and non-durable goods are flat; unemployment remains below the euro-area average (6.5%), but has risen to 6.3% from 5.6% in May.

The energy price rise has only just begun to feed through to food prices, not yet to other goods, but it has already pushed the ECB and the Fed to raise rates, and government bond yields have risen by about 0.8 points in a year: more interest on debt, fewer public resources to support households and investment. Black Friday and Christmas should support year-end purchases; the bill may arrive in 2027. For companies the priority becomes profitability. On the public side it is reasonable to expect support for low-income households and energy-intensive firms and, in the medium term, investment in energy efficiency, including with private capital, to reduce exposure to shocks like the one we are going through in 2026.

Three pieces of evidence

  • Weak growth in Europe, flat in Italy. Excluding fuel, in the July–September 2026 average euro-area retail sales grew year on year by 2.5% in value and 1.2% in volume; in Italy +1.0% against −0.5%, in Germany +1.2% against −1.4%. In the third quarter Italian GDP is flat (0.0% on the previous quarter), while the euro area grows by 0.4%; in Spain volumes slow to +0.6%.
  • Confidence down, purchase intentions at the year's low. In October consumer confidence falls to −17.9 in the euro area (−1.1 points vs September), to −21.9 in France (from −18.5) and to −23.3 in Italy, the lowest balance among the large countries. In Italy the Findomestic purchase-intentions index loses 4.4% on September, to the lowest in a year, and households able to save rise to 59% from 55%.
  • In Europe price is pushing less. In the third quarter Nestlé grows 1.2% in Europe with almost flat prices (+0.2%), Unilever slips (−1.7%) with falling prices (−1.3%). In luxury, in the first half of 2026, Europe grows less than the Americas in all 6 companies that report both figures; the same holds for Adidas and Hugo Boss.

Three implications for consumer companies

  • In Europe the price lever is running out: a sign, not a rule. In the third quarter Nestlé's price growth in Europe falls to 0.2%, from 2.2% in the first half, with volumes at +1.0%; Unilever declines with falling prices (−1.3%) and still-negative volumes (−0.4%). Two cases do not make a law, but with food prices around 1% in the euro area (+1.0% in September) and purchase intentions falling, a list-price increase risks being paid for in volumes: better to work on price architecture and pack formats.
  • Reallocate by channel, verify by country. Online grows faster than the total everywhere. Excluding fuel, in the July–September average volumes grow in France (+3.4%) and slow in Spain (+0.6%), while they fall in Italy (−0.5%) and especially in Germany (−1.4%): a signal to confirm with the Christmas season before shifting budget between countries. Across channels, instead, the budget should be reallocated, not cut.
  • In luxury the brand seems to matter more than the market. In Europe Hermès (+8.8%, France excluded) and Cucinelli (+5.3%) grow, Zegna is almost flat (+1.5%); Moncler and Prada (−4%) and Ferragamo (−8.6%) decline. Six companies with different perimeters: an indication of trend, not a ranking.

Implications by category

How to read. For each category: the context in the data, the three levers to act on over the next three months (price, offer, channel) and the threshold that would change the recommendation.

Food and beverages

Context. Food prices +1.7% in Italy in September, from 1.1% in August (in October, Istat flash estimate: shopping basket +2.5%, unprocessed food +7.5%), food volumes −0.3% in the July–September average. In Europe, in the third quarter, Nestlé grows 1.2% with almost flat prices (+0.2%), Unilever slips 1.7% with falling prices (−1.3%).

Price

With food prices at +1.0% in the euro area in September, a price list that runs ahead of the category is paid for in volumes: grow with mix and innovation, not with across-the-board increases.

Offer

Entry-level formats and multipacks to recover Italian food volumes (−0.3%); reduce promotions where they do not generate incremental volumes.

Channel

Private label now competes on quality too: brand difference must be demonstrated on the shelf, not just claimed.

Threshold to monitor

Euro-area food inflation above 2% (today 1.0%): room would reopen to adjust list prices.

Beauty and personal care

Context. It is the most resilient category: L'Oréal +6.8% in the half year and +7.2% in the third quarter (Europe +9.9%), personal-care prices +1.1% in Italy, health and beauty the first online category (+24% in 2025).

Price

Premium with proven benefit supports price and mix (L'Oréal +6.8% in the half year).

Offer

Invest in efficacy and scientific communication, which justify the price more than the packaging.

Channel

Cover marketplaces and social commerce: health and beauty is the first online category in Italy (+24% in 2025).

Threshold to monitor

Euro-area online growth close to the total (gap below 2 points, today 2.8): channel growth stops taking share from physical stores.

Fashion and luxury

Context. Clothing prices flat (0.0% in Italy), non-food −0.6% in volume in Italy (July–September average). In luxury in Europe Hermès and Cucinelli grow, Zegna is almost flat, Ferragamo, Moncler and Prada decline.

Price

Clothing prices flat (0.0% in Italy): little room for price increases outside luxury.

Offer

Review the assortment towards the entry price of the range, where the remaining demand is concentrated.

Channel and markets

United States before Europe: the Americas grow more than Europe in all luxury companies that report the figure. Reduce dependence on wholesale, where declines are concentrated (Ferragamo).

Threshold to monitor

Euro-area consumer confidence below −18 for two months (today −17.9 in October, after −16.8 in September): plan the Christmas season on cautious volumes.

Durables and cars

Context. Durables growing in Italy (+0.9% in Q2), European registrations +6.3% between January and September (Italy +8.6%), with Chinese brands growing strongly; but in October purchase intentions for new cars (12%, −0.7 points) and furniture (22%, −2.1) fall, the ECB is at 2.50% and may rise further, and the purchasing power of Italian households fell by 0.9% in Q2.

Price

Defend the price-to-equipment ratio against Chinese new entrants (BYD +135% between January and September, share 2.7%), without chasing them on price.

Offer

Consumer finance and deferred payments to offset the cost of credit, with the ECB at 2.50%.

Channel and markets

In Italy durables are the goods that grow most in household spending (+0.9% in Q2): a market to defend, not to scale down.

Threshold to monitor

New ECB hike at the December meeting: review at once the financing terms offered to customers.

The reading of the month

While total sales grow little, online grows 4% in Italy and between 3% and 6% in the other large euro-area countries. For those who lead a consumer brand the question is not whether to invest in digital, but where the margin is shifting: marketplaces capture the growth, acquisition costs rise and visibility in the catalogues read by AI assistants becomes a new distribution lever. Those who control the direct channel and first-party data today defend the margin tomorrow.

FX and government bonds

Sources: ECB, Deutsche Bundesbank, US Treasury.

Why they matter for consumption

Yields. When government bond yields rise, especially on long maturities, the State pays more interest on the debt it rolls over: fewer resources remain for investment and for support measures for households. With debt at 138.1% of GDP in 2026*, for Italy every increase weighs more than elsewhere. Exchange rates. A weaker euro makes imports in dollars and yuan (energy, raw materials, finished goods) more expensive and European exports more competitive; a stronger euro does the opposite.

In September 10-year yields rise again: +0.8 points in a year in Italy, +0.9 in France.

How to read the comparisons between companies

Same period: all comparisons use the first half of 2026 (January–June). Companies whose financial year is not aligned with the calendar year (Richemont, Burberry, Inditex, Diageo) are excluded, as their latest figures cover different periods. Different metrics: organic growth, at constant exchange rates, like-for-like or comparable, according to each company's definition. Different perimeters: the "Europe" of Moncler, Pandora, Hugo Boss and Zegna is EMEA (includes the Middle East and Africa), Hermès' excludes France; Hermès' Asia excludes Japan, Ferragamo's is Asia Pacific, Zegna's is Greater China (rest of Asia-Pacific +13.6%). For this reason the comparisons indicate trends, not rankings.

    Methodology notes

    How thresholds are set

    Each threshold follows one of four criteria, stated line by line: institutional reference (an official target, for example the ECB's 2%); revision margin (zero plus the margin within which the figure is normally revised, so as not to react to noise); historical band (a return to the levels of a recent documented episode, confirmed for two months); dynamics (a widening or halving compared with the last figure). Thresholds are revised only when the criterion changes, not when the figure does.

    Period rule

    Retail sales (total, food, non-food, online): average of the last three available months against the same period a year earlier, always excluding fuel, because the monthly figure is volatile. Prices, confidence, unemployment and rates: latest available month. Company results: first half of the calendar year; companies whose financial year is not aligned are excluded. Exchange rates and government bond yields: latest available daily figure compared with the same date a year earlier; for Italy, France and Spain the ECB harmonised monthly average. Registrations: cumulative year to date against the same period a year earlier. Forecasts: latest official publication, with date. The page is updated on the first day of each month.

    Sources and method

    Method

    • Primary sources for the numbers: statistical institutes, central banks, European Commission, OECD, IMF, company accounts and releases. News outlets are used for the news items.
    • Double check: where the official release is not accessible, the figure is published only if two authoritative outlets report identical values, with a visible label.
    • Official forecasts only, with publication date. No statistical projections of my own.
    • Period rule: sales as three-month average excluding fuel, prices and confidence at the latest month, companies at first half (details in the methodology notes).
    • Period always stated and company definitions reported in their own terms (for example RIG, USG, like-for-like).
    • Revisions tracked: the database keeps the history of the values downloaded.
    • Updates: automatic daily collection of data (Eurostat, ONS, BLS, University of Michigan, SEC); page edition on the first day of each month.
